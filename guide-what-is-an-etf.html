What is an ETF?

One purchase, hundreds of companies. ETFs are the simplest way to own a whole market, and the easiest thing to misunderstand. Here is how they work and what to check before buying one.

Funds and index investing · 7 min read · for UK beginners · checked 21 November 2026

What you are buying

An ETF, an exchange-traded fund, is a fund that holds a basket of investments and is itself listed on a stock exchange, so you buy and sell it like a share, at a price that moves through the day. Most ETFs are index trackers: a FTSE 100 ETF holds the 100 companies in the index, in the index's proportions, and tries to match its return. Buy one unit and you own a slice of every company inside.

ETF or fund?

A traditional index fund (an OEIC or unit trust in the UK) does the same job but is priced once a day and bought from the fund manager. An ETF trades on the exchange with a bid and an ask, so there is a spread every time you deal, and some brokers charge a dealing fee for ETFs but not for funds. For small regular amounts that difference can matter more than the ongoing charge.

The three things on the factsheet that matter

  • What it tracks. The index in the name. "FTSE All-World", "S&P 500" and "FTSE 100" are three very different baskets. The factsheet lists the top holdings and the country and sector split, which tells you what you are really exposed to.
  • The ongoing charge (OCF). The fund's yearly cost, taken out of its value, shown as a percentage. 0.1% on £1,000 is £1 a year. Large, plain index ETFs are cheap; narrow or clever ones cost more.
  • Accumulating or distributing. A distributing ETF pays the dividends of its holdings out to you as cash. An accumulating one reinvests them inside the fund, so the price drifts up instead. Same investments, different plumbing. Inside an ISA neither is taxed; outside, both have tax consequences.

Things that surprise beginners

  • Currency. An ETF that holds US companies is exposed to the dollar whatever currency it is quoted in. If the pound strengthens, your sterling value falls even if the companies did fine. Some ETFs hedge this; they say so in the name.
  • Tracking difference. No ETF matches its index exactly. The gap, usually small, is the charge plus the friction of running the fund. It is on the factsheet as a comparison of fund and index returns.
  • Leveraged and inverse ETFs. Some products are built to deliver two or three times a daily move, or the opposite of it. They reset every day, so over weeks they do not behave the way the name suggests. They are trading tools, not something to hold.
  • UCITS. Most ETFs sold to UK investors carry this label, which means they follow a European rule set on diversification and disclosure. Many popular US-listed ETFs cannot be bought by UK retail investors for that reason.

Why traders learn ETFs too

In Pulse, ETFs appear on day one because they are how you trade a whole index, sector or commodity with one order. The lesson Indices and ETFs explains the difference between an index and the fund that tracks it, and the paper account lets you buy one with virtual money to watch how it moves against the index it follows.

Remember this

  • An ETF is a fund that trades on the exchange like a share and usually tracks an index.
  • Read three things first: what it tracks, the ongoing charge, and whether it pays out or reinvests dividends.
  • Currency and tracking difference explain most gaps between the fund and the headline index.
  • Leveraged and inverse ETFs reset daily and are not for holding.
