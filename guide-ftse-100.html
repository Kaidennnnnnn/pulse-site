What is the FTSE 100?

It is on every news bulletin when markets move. Here is what the number actually measures, who decides which companies are in it, and what it means to invest in it.

UK stock market · 6 min read · for UK beginners · checked 21 November 2026

What the number is

The FTSE 100 (said "footsie") is an index: one number that summarises the share prices of the 100 largest companies listed on the London Stock Exchange, measured by market value. When the news says "the FTSE 100 closed up 1%", it means that basket of 100 companies, taken together, was worth about 1% more at the close than the day before.

It started on 3 January 1984 at a level of 1,000. Every level since is relative to that starting point, which is why the number itself (8,000, 9,000) means nothing on its own. The change is what matters.

Who runs it

The index is calculated and maintained by FTSE Russell, which is part of the London Stock Exchange Group. FTSE Russell does not pick companies it likes. Membership follows published rules, mostly about size, so that the index stays objective.

How a company gets in, or falls out

Four times a year, in March, June, September and December, FTSE Russell reviews the list. Companies that have grown large enough are promoted from the FTSE 250 (the next 250 companies by size); companies that have shrunk are relegated. There are buffer rules so that a company does not bounce in and out on a small move. In 2026, for example, FTSE Russell announced that IG Group and Lion Finance joined in March, Aberdeen Group, Computacenter and Investec joined in June, and easyJet and Ithaca Energy joined in September, each time with the same number of companies moving down to the FTSE 250.

These names are given as examples of how the reshuffle works, not as anything to buy. By the time you read this the list will have changed again; the current constituents are published by FTSE Russell.

Why the biggest companies dominate

The FTSE 100 is weighted by market value. A company worth £150 billion moves the index far more than one worth £5 billion, so a handful of banks, energy and pharmaceutical groups set much of the day's direction. The index is also international: many of its companies earn most of their money abroad, in dollars. That is why the FTSE 100 often rises when the pound falls, which surprises people. A weaker pound makes those overseas earnings worth more in sterling.

"Investing in the FTSE 100"

You cannot buy an index directly. What people buy is a fund or ETF that holds the same 100 companies in the same proportions, so its value follows the index closely. Such a fund has an ongoing charge, usually small for a large index, and it pays out the dividends of the companies it holds, or reinvests them. What is an ETF? covers how that works.

Tracking the FTSE 100 means accepting its mix: heavy in finance, energy and consumer staples, light in technology compared with the US indices. Whether that mix suits anyone is a question about their own goals, not about the index.

Reading the FTSE 100 as a trader

In the Pulse Academy the index is one of the first charts you read. A 1% daily move is a normal day; 3% is a big one; the 10% falls of March 2020 are the kind of day that teaches what a stop-loss is for. The free lesson Indices and ETFs explains why "the market fell 2%" always needs the question "which market?".

Remember this

  • The FTSE 100 is the 100 largest London-listed companies, weighted by market value, as one number.
  • FTSE Russell reviews membership every quarter by published rules. Companies move between it and the FTSE 250.
  • Its companies earn much of their money abroad, so a weaker pound often lifts the index.
  • You invest in it through an index fund or ETF that holds the same companies.
