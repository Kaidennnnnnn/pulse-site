Dividends explained

A dividend is a company handing part of its profit to the people who own it. Here is how it is paid, why the share price drops on the day, and how UK tax treats it.

Shares · 6 min read · for UK beginners · checked 21 November 2026

What a dividend is

When a company makes a profit it can keep the money to grow, or pay some of it out to shareholders. The payment is a dividend, set as an amount per share. Own 100 shares and a 20p dividend is £20. Companies that pay are usually mature ones with steady profits; fast-growing companies often pay nothing and reinvest everything, which is not a flaw.

The four dates

  • Declaration date. The company announces the dividend, the amount and the dates below.
  • Ex-dividend date. The cut-off. Buy the share before this day and you get the dividend; buy on or after it and you do not. On this morning the share price usually drops by roughly the dividend, because the cash is about to leave the company. Nobody gets a free dividend by buying the day before.
  • Record date. The day the company checks its register of owners, normally the business day after the ex-date.
  • Payment date. The money arrives, often weeks later.

Dividend yield

Yield is the yearly dividend divided by the share price. A share at £10 paying 40p a year yields 4%. The number tempts beginners, so two cautions. A high yield can mean the price has fallen because the market expects the dividend to be cut, which makes the yield a warning rather than a bargain. And a yield is not an interest rate: the dividend can be reduced or stopped at any time, and the price can fall by more than the income.

Reinvesting

Dividends paid as cash sit in your account until you do something with them. Many brokers can reinvest them automatically, and accumulating funds do it inside the fund. Over years, reinvested dividends are a large part of the total return of the stock market, which is the arithmetic behind "compounding" and is the subject of the Lab's savings tool.

UK tax, in brief

For 2026/27 the first £500 of dividend income in a tax year is covered by the dividend allowance and is tax-free. Above that, dividends are taxed at a rate that depends on your income tax band; the current rates are on gov.uk. Dividends from shares held inside a Stocks and Shares ISA are not taxed and are not reported. Dividends from a pension are not taxed either while inside the pension. This is a summary of public rules, not tax advice; the figures were checked on the date at the bottom of this page.

In the practice account

Pulse's paper trading pays simulated dividends on positions held across an ex-date and drops the price on the day, so you can see the mechanics happen without owning anything. The accuracy page lists exactly what is simulated.

Remember this

  • A dividend is profit paid out per share. Growth companies often pay none, and that is normal.
  • You must own the share before the ex-dividend date. The price drops by about the dividend that morning.
  • A high yield can be a warning that the market expects a cut.
  • The first £500 of dividends a year is tax-free for 2026/27; inside an ISA, all of it is.
