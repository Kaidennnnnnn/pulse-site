Paper trading: how to practise without money

Pilots use simulators. Traders have paper accounts. Here is what practising with virtual money can teach you, what it cannot, and how to do it so the lessons carry over.

Practice · 6 min read · for UK beginners · checked 21 November 2026

What it is

Paper trading is placing trades with pretend money on real or simulated prices, so you can learn the mechanics and your own reactions before any real money is involved. The name comes from the days when people wrote trades on paper and checked the price later. Today it is an account in an app with a balance that is not real.

What it teaches well

  • The mechanics. Market and limit orders, stops, what "filled" means, why the price you get is not the price you saw. Making those mistakes with virtual money costs a minute. Making them with real money costs the money.
  • Position sizing. How many shares a trade should be, given what you are prepared to lose. This is arithmetic, and the paper account is where it becomes a habit. The calculator shows the formula.
  • What a plan feels like. Entering with a stop and a target written down, and then watching whether you keep to it.
  • How markets actually move. Gaps, spreads, a quiet week followed by a wild day. Charts in a book never prepare anyone for the real speed.

What it cannot teach

Fear. When the money is not real, a 10% loss is a number. When it is real, the same loss changes how you breathe, and most trading mistakes are made in that state. A paper account also makes it easy to be careless: oversize a trade, ignore a stop, reset the balance. The gap between paper results and real results is well known, and Pulse's lessons say so plainly rather than pretending the practice account is a crystal ball.

Six rules that make it count

  1. Use a balance you could actually have. £10,000 of virtual money teaches nothing if you would start with £500.
  2. Risk a fixed small share per trade, 1% or 2%, and never change it because you feel confident.
  3. Write the reason and the stop before you enter. If you cannot, do not enter.
  4. Never reset the account after a bad run. The bad run is the lesson.
  5. Keep a journal of every trade: reason, stop, result, what you felt. Patterns show up after twenty trades.
  6. Count trades, not weeks. Fifty planned trades teach more than a year of occasional ones.

How Pulse does it

The Pulse practice account uses real market prices (which can be delayed), charges a spread on every fill, lets stops gap through bad news, works out size from your risk rule, and keeps a journal that reports your habits back to you. The browser simulator follows the same rules on a simulated market you can fast-forward, so you can see a whole trade play out in a minute. Nothing in either is real, and nothing in Pulse ever places a real trade.

Remember this

  • Paper trading teaches mechanics, sizing and planning. It cannot teach what a real loss feels like.
  • Practise with a balance you could really have, and a fixed small risk per trade.
  • Never reset after a losing streak. Write every trade down.
  • Judge yourself on whether you kept the plan, not on the virtual profit.
