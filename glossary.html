Pulse / Glossary

The trading glossary.

1114 terms from the Pulse Academy, each explained in a sentence or two. From ask and bid to yield curve and Z-score.

1114 terms
#ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ

#

12-1 momentum
The return over the past twelve months excluding the most recent month, a common momentum measure.
30-day rule
The UK rule, also called bed and breakfasting, that matches a sale with the same shares bought back within 30 days, so a quick sell-and-rebuy cannot create a paper loss.
4% rule
A rule of thumb for withdrawing 4% of a starting portfolio in year one and adjusting the amount for inflation after that.
401(k)
A US workplace retirement plan that takes contributions from pay, often with an employer match.
50/30/20 rule
A budgeting guideline that puts about 50% of take-home pay towards needs, 30% towards wants and 20% towards saving and debt repayment.

A

A-shares
Shares of mainland Chinese companies listed in Shanghai or Shenzhen and priced in yuan.
Absorption
When heavy aggressive orders hit a price but it barely moves, because a large passive trader takes the other side.
Accruals ratio
Net income minus operating cash flow, divided by average total assets, showing how much profit is non-cash.
Accumulating and distributing
Two versions of a fund. Accumulating (Acc) reinvests dividends inside the fund; distributing (Dist) pays them out to investors as cash.
Accumulation units
A version of a fund that automatically reinvests dividends instead of paying them out. The paying-out version is called income units.
Accumulation/distribution line
A running total of volume weighted by where each close sits in the bar's range: full volume is added at the high and subtracted at the low.
Action list
The short part of a watchlist holding stocks close to a planned entry, each with its entry, stop and earnings date noted.
Active return
A portfolio's return minus its benchmark's return.
Activity cost
The spread, commission and financing a copied account pays because its trader makes many trades.
Add-back
A cost excluded from adjusted profit on the grounds that it is unusual or non-cash.
Adjusted net debt
Reported net debt plus debt-like items such as leases, pension deficits and guarantees.
Adjusted prices
Historical prices restated to account for splits and dividends so that comparisons over time are valid.
ADR
An American depositary receipt: a US-traded certificate that represents shares of a foreign company.
Advance-fee fraud
A scam that asks for a payment up front to release a larger sum that does not exist.
Adverse selection
The risk of trading with someone better informed, so the price tends to move against you right after the trade.
ADX (Average Directional Index)
A 0 to 100 measure of trend strength that ignores direction. Readings above about 25 are often treated as a trending market.
Affinity fraud
A scam that targets a group bound by trust, such as a community, church or club.
Aggressor
The trader in a transaction who crossed the spread with a market order, paying the offer to buy or hitting the bid to sell.
Airdrop
A free distribution of tokens, which scammers use as bait for malicious approvals.
Alarm level
A pre-set drawdown or loss that triggers a review or an end to a copy.
All Weather
Ray Dalio's portfolio idea of balancing assets so that no single economic environment dominates results.
Allocation
The amount of money you assign to copy one trader.
Alpha
The return a portfolio earns beyond what its exposure to the market (beta) would predict.
Alternative investment
An investment outside traditional listed shares, bonds and cash, such as private equity, hedge funds or real assets.
American option
An option that can be exercised at any time up to and including expiry.
AML
Anti-money laundering: rules and processes designed to stop the proceeds of crime from entering the financial system.
Anchoring
Relying too heavily on an initial number, such as a past price or your entry price, when making a judgement. Later estimates get pulled towards the anchor.
Annual exempt amount
The UK tax-free allowance for capital gains each tax year. Gains above it may be taxed; the government sets the figure and has changed it often.
Annuity
An insurance contract that exchanges a sum of money for a regular income, often for life.
API key
A credential that lets software trade or read an account, whose permissions should be limited to what is needed.
Appraisal smoothing
The tendency of valuation-based returns to look less volatile than true market values.
APR
Annual percentage rate: the yearly cost of borrowing, shown as a percentage of the amount borrowed.
APY (AER)
Annual percentage yield, called AER in the UK: the effective yearly rate on savings after compounding is included.
Ask
The lowest price a seller will accept right now. You buy at the ask.
Asset allocation
How you split your money between types of asset, such as stocks, bonds and cash. It drives most of a portfolio's risk.
Asset location
Choosing which investments to hold in which type of account in order to reduce tax.
Asset play
A company whose shares trade below the value of assets it owns, in Peter Lynch's classification.
Assignment
The notice that an option seller must fulfil the contract: deliver shares for a short call, or buy them for a short put.
Asymmetric bet
A bet in which the possible gain is much larger than the possible loss.
AT1 bonds
Additional tier 1 bonds, risky bank bonds that can be written down or converted to equity when a bank's capital falls too low.
ATR
Average True Range: the average size of each bar's move, including gaps. Used for stops and sizing.
ATR (Average True Range)
A measure of the typical size of price movement over a period, used to set stops and position sizes.
Authenticator app
An app that creates short-lived login codes on your own device.
Authorised participant
A large firm, usually a bank or market maker, allowed to create and redeem ETF shares directly with the fund.
Auto-deleveraging (ADL)
A last-resort system that forces profitable traders to close positions when an exchange's insurance fund cannot cover losses.
Auto-enrolment
A system that enrols employees in a workplace pension automatically unless they opt out.
Autocallable
A note that is redeemed early with a coupon if the underlying is at or above a trigger level on an observation date.
Autocorrelation
The correlation of a series with its own past values.
Automated market maker (AMM)
A type of decentralised exchange that prices trades from a pool of tokens and a formula instead of an order book.
Average daily volume (ADV)
The number of shares that typically trade in a day, used to judge liquidity and how long an exit would take.
Average true range (N)
A measure of typical daily price movement used by the Turtles to size positions and set stops.

B

Backtest
A simulation that applies a system's rules to past data to estimate how it would have performed. It is only as honest as its data and assumptions.
Backwardation
A futures curve where later contracts trade below nearer ones, often a sign of tight current supply.
Balance transfer
Moving a debt to another card or lender, often to take advantage of a low or 0% introductory rate.
Bank run
A rush by depositors to withdraw money because they fear the bank may fail.
Base
A sideways price range after a rise, where a stock consolidates its gains before its next move.
Base currency
The first currency in a pair, such as the euro in EUR/USD. The price shows how much of the second currency one unit of it costs.
Base rate
How common an event is in general, before considering specific evidence.
Basis
The difference between the local cash price of a commodity and the futures price.
Basis point
One hundredth of a percentage point, or 0.01%. A rise from 4.00% to 4.25% is 25 basis points.
Basis trade
A strategy that buys the asset and sells the matching derivative to collect the price gap or funding while staying market-neutral.
Bear market
A broad decline, often defined as 20% or more from a recent high.
Bear market rally
A sharp rise within a longer downtrend that fails to reverse it.
Beat and raise
A report where results top expectations and the company also lifts its forecast. It is the classic strong earnings outcome.
Bed and ISA
Selling investments in a general account and buying them back inside an ISA, so future growth is sheltered. The sale itself can create a taxable gain.
Behaviour tag
A label on a trade recording your state of mind or circumstances, such as FOMO or tiredness.
Behavioural finance
The study of how psychology affects financial decisions and market prices. It explains why real people and markets often depart from purely rational models.
Best execution
The duty of a broker to seek the best overall result for a client's order, considering price, cost, speed and likelihood of execution.
Beta
How much a stock tends to move relative to the market. Beta 1.5 moves about 1.5 times as much.
Beta-weighted exposure
A position's value multiplied by its beta, giving its equivalent exposure to the index. Added up across a book, it sets the size of an index hedge.
Bid
The highest price a buyer will pay right now. You sell at the bid.
Big bath
A large write-off taken in one period, usually after a management change, so that later profits look better.
Bill-and-hold
Recording a sale before the goods have been shipped to the customer.
Binary catalyst
A news event with two sharply different outcomes, such as a drug trial result, which can cause a large price gap.
Binary event
An event with two sharply different outcomes, such as an approval or a refusal.
Bitcoin dominance
Bitcoin's market cap as a share of the total crypto market cap. Falling dominance often means money is moving into altcoins.
Black Wednesday
16 September 1992, the day Britain left the European exchange rate mechanism and the pound fell sharply.
Boiler room
A high-pressure telephone sales operation that pushes worthless or overpriced investments.
Bollinger Bands
Bands two standard deviations around a 20-period average, showing volatility.
Bollinger squeeze
A period of unusually low volatility, often defined as Bollinger Bands contracting inside a Keltner channel. It hints at a bigger move without saying which way.
Bond ladder
A set of bonds that mature at regular steps, such as once a year. Each maturing bond is spent or reinvested at the far end.
Bond proxy
A share, often a utility or high-dividend stock, that investors value like a bond because of its steady income.
Bonus certificate
A certificate that pays at least a bonus level at maturity unless a barrier is touched.
Book value
Total assets minus total liabilities, also called shareholders' equity. An accounting figure, not what the company would sell for.
Borrow fee
The interest a short seller pays to borrow shares. It rises when shares are hard to borrow.
Bracket order
An order that places an entry together with a stop-loss and a profit target.
Break of structure
When price moves beyond the most recent swing high or swing low. In the trend's direction it confirms the trend; against it, it is an early warning of change.
Break-even occupancy
The share of time a property must be let for rent to cover operating costs and debt payments.
Breakeven inflation
The gap between a normal government bond yield and an inflation-linked bond yield of the same maturity. It is a market estimate of expected inflation.
Breakeven win rate
The win rate at which a strategy makes no profit or loss: 1 divided by (1 plus the reward to risk ratio).
Breakout
Price closing beyond a level that previously held it, ideally on high volume.
Breakout pivot
The price at the top of a base's final tight area. A move above it is the trigger for a breakout entry.
Brier score
The average squared difference between forecast probabilities and outcomes, where lower is better.
Broad money
A wide measure of the money supply that includes cash, current accounts, savings deposits and other near-money.
Budget
A plan that compares your income with your spending and decides in advance where each pound will go.
Bull market
A broad, sustained rise in prices.
Burn rate
The amount of cash a company uses each month in excess of what it brings in.
Business cycle
The recurring pattern of expansion, peak, recession and recovery in economic activity.
Buy stop-limit order
An order that triggers at a stop price but fills only up to a set limit price. It caps the price paid but may not fill on a big gap.
Buyback
When a company uses cash to purchase its own shares, reducing the share count.
Buyer's premium
A fee added to the winning bid at auction, paid by the buyer.
Buying on margin
Buying investments partly with money borrowed from a broker. It magnifies both gains and losses.

C

Calibration
The match between how confident you say you are and how often you turn out to be right.
Call spread
A position that buys one call and sells another at a different strike, limiting both gain and loss.
Candidate list
The names a screen returns, to be researched before any decision.
Candlestick
A bar showing open, high, low and close for a period.
Capacity
The amount of capital a strategy can handle before its own trading reduces its edge.
Capital call
A request by a private fund for investors to send part of the money they have committed.
Capital controls
Government rules that limit the movement of money into or out of a country.
Capital cycle
The pattern in which high prices attract investment and new supply, which later pushes prices down, and the reverse.
Capital protection
A promise by the issuer to return all or part of the invested amount at maturity.
Capitalisation rate
Net operating income divided by the property's value, used to compare income properties.
Capitalised cost
Spending recorded as an asset and expensed gradually through depreciation, instead of being expensed at once.
CAPM
The Capital Asset Pricing Model: the cost of equity equals the risk-free rate plus beta times the equity risk premium.
Carried interest
The share of a private fund's profits, usually 20%, paid to the manager after investors have received their hurdle.
Carry trade
Borrowing in a low-interest currency to hold a higher-interest one, earning the difference as long as exchange rates stay calm.
Cash conversion
Operating cash flow divided by net income, showing how much profit turns into cash.
Cash runway
The number of months a company can operate on its cash at its current rate of burn.
Cash settlement
Settlement of an option or future by paying the cash value of the difference instead of delivering the underlying.
Catalyst
The news or event behind a stock's move, such as earnings, guidance, an analyst upgrade or a takeover.
Catch-up
A term that lets the manager receive most or all profits after the hurdle until it holds its agreed share of the total.
Central bank
The public body, such as the Bank of England or the US Federal Reserve, that sets interest rates and manages a country's money.
Central bank gold buying
Purchases of gold by national central banks to hold in their reserves.
Central counterparty (CCP)
An institution that becomes the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer, reducing counterparty risk.
Centralised exchange (CEX)
A trading venue run by a company that matches orders and holds customers' coins in its own wallets.
CEO pay ratio
The ratio of the chief executive's total pay to the median employee's pay, which large US companies must disclose.
CET1 ratio
A bank's highest-quality capital, mainly shares and retained profits, divided by its risk-weighted assets.
CFD
Contract for difference: a leveraged product that tracks a price without owning the asset. Common in the UK for shorting.
Channel stuffing
Shipping more goods to distributors than end customers will buy, in order to book sales early.
Checklist
A short list of routine steps that are done every time to prevent avoidable errors.
Cherry-picking
Showing only the best results while leaving out the rest.
Chokepoint
A narrow passage, such as a canal or strait, through which a large share of trade must pass.
Cigar-butt investing
Buying very cheap, struggling companies for the last bit of value left in them.
CIK
The Central Index Key, a unique number the SEC assigns to each filer.
Circle of competence
The set of businesses and industries that an investor understands well enough to judge.
Circuit breaker
An automatic trading pause triggered when prices fall by a set amount, meant to give people time to think.
Clawback
A provision requiring a fund manager to return carried interest if later losses mean it was overpaid.
Clone firm
A scam that copies the name, address or registration details of a genuine authorised firm to appear legitimate.
Cloned firm
A scam company that copies the name, logo or registration details of a genuine regulated firm.
Close location
Where a candle's close sits within its high-to-low range, often shown as a percentage. Near 100% means buyers finished in control; near 0% means sellers did.
Closed-end fund
A fund with a fixed number of shares that trade on the market, often away from net asset value.
Closing auction
A process that collects orders before the close and matches them at one price, which becomes the official closing price. Exchanges publish the imbalance, the unmatched shares, beforehand.
Cointegration
A statistical relationship in which the spread between two assets tends to return to an average over time.
Cold wallet
A wallet that keeps private keys offline, often on a hardware device, to reduce hacking risk.
Collar
A protective put combined with a short call, limiting both downside and upside.
Commitments of Traders (COT) report
A weekly report from the CFTC, the US futures regulator, showing how groups such as producers and money managers are positioned in futures markets.
Comparable companies
Similar listed businesses whose valuation multiples are used to estimate the value of another company.
Compensation discussion and analysis
A section of the proxy statement explaining how and why executives are paid.
Composite score
A single ranking number built by combining several measures, such as momentum and earnings growth.
Compounding
Earning returns on your past returns as well as on the money you put in. Over long periods it makes growth speed up.
Compression
A period when candles shrink and price squeezes into a tight area. It often comes before a larger move, in either direction.
Conditional probability
The probability of one event given that another has already occurred.
Confidence range
A range around an estimate, such as the average plus or minus two standard errors, that is likely to contain the true value.
Confidence score
A figure showing how strongly the evidence agrees, which is not automatically the probability of profit.
Confirmation bias
Seeking, noticing and believing information that supports your existing view while discounting evidence against it.
Conflict of interest
A situation in which a person's or firm's interests could influence advice or actions against a client's interest.
Confluence
When several independent levels or signals line up at the same price, such as a Fibonacci level, old support and a moving average. It makes a level more noteworthy.
Conglomerate discount
The amount by which the market value of a diversified group falls below the sum of its parts.
Consensus estimate
The average or median of published analyst forecasts for a figure such as EPS or revenue. It is the usual benchmark for a beat or a miss.
Consensus forecast
The average expectation of economists for a data release. Markets react to the gap between the actual figure and this forecast.
Consistency rule
A rule limiting how much of your total profit may come from a single day or trade. It is meant to filter out lucky gambles.
Constant-maturity
A measure that interpolates between expiries to keep a fixed time to expiry, such as 30 days.
Contango
A futures curve where later contracts trade above nearer ones. Rolling a long position in contango tends to cost money.
Contingency
An allowance added to a budget to cover unexpected costs.
Contract for difference (CFD)
An agreement with a broker to exchange the change in an asset's price, giving leveraged exposure without owning the asset.
Contract multiplier
The number of shares one option contract covers. For US stock options it is 100.
Contract size
The amount of the underlying asset covered by one futures contract, such as 1,000 barrels of oil.
Control premium
The extra amount an acquirer pays above the market price to gain control of a company.
Convenience yield
The benefit of having a commodity on hand right now, highest during shortages. A high convenience yield can push the futures curve into backwardation.
Convexity
The curve in the link between a bond's price and its yield. For ordinary bonds, prices fall less and rise more than duration alone predicts.
Cookie-jar reserve
A reserve built up in good years and released in bad years to smooth reported earnings.
Cooling-off period
A deliberate pause before a financial decision, which removes the scammer's urgency.
Cooling-off rule
A personal rule to pause trading for a set time or after a set number of losses.
Copied trader
The person whose trades are replicated in your account.
Copier
An investor who follows and replicates another trader's trades.
Copy trading
A service that opens and closes trades in your account to match a chosen trader, in proportion to your allocation.
Core and satellite
A portfolio design with a large low-cost core and a few smaller, more targeted holdings.
Core inflation
Inflation excluding volatile food and energy prices, used to show the underlying trend.
Core-satellite
A portfolio with a low-cost market core and smaller allocations to specific styles or themes.
Correlation
How closely two assets move together, from -1 (opposite) to +1 (identical).
Correlation matrix
A table showing the correlation between every pair of holdings in a portfolio.
Cost basis
The amount paid for an investment including fees, used to work out gains and losses for tax.
Cost curve
A ranking of producers from lowest to highest cost, used to see who earns money at a given price.
Cost matters hypothesis
Bogle's argument that, after costs, the average active investor must earn less than the average index investor.
Cost of carry
The cost of holding a commodity until a later date: storage, insurance and financing. It helps explain why later futures often trade above spot.
Counterparty risk
The risk that the other party in a trade or the institution holding your assets fails to meet its obligations.
Country risk premium
The extra return investors demand to hold assets in a country with higher political, economic or legal risk.
Coupon
The fixed interest a bond pays, written as a yearly percentage of face value. Many bonds pay it in two halves each year.
Covered call fund
A fund that holds shares and sells call options on them to earn extra income, giving up some upside.
Covered warrant
A warrant issued by a bank on existing assets, which does not create new shares.
CPI
The Consumer Price Index: the cost of a fixed basket of goods and services bought by households, the most common measure of inflation.
Creation unit
The large block of ETF shares that an authorised participant creates or redeems in one go, in exchange for the underlying basket or cash.
Credit cycle
The recurring pattern of expansion and contraction in lending, driven by standards, confidence and asset values.
Credit default swap (CDS)
A contract that pays out if a borrower defaults, whose price shows how the market rates default risk.
Credit freeze
A moment when banks stop lending to each other and to businesses because they no longer trust who can repay.
Credit rating
An assessment by a rating agency of a borrower's ability to repay its debts.
Credit spread
A spread that sells a nearer option and buys a further one, collecting money up front. Its maximum loss is the strike width minus the credit.
Critical accounting estimates
Accounting judgements where reasonable alternative assumptions would materially change the reported numbers.
Cross margin
A mode in which the whole account balance backs all open positions.
Cross pair
A pair of two major currencies that does not include the US dollar, such as EUR/GBP or EUR/JPY.
Crowded trade
A position held by so many traders that few new buyers or sellers remain, making it vulnerable to sharp reversals.
Cup and handle
A continuation pattern: a rounded base followed by a small, shallow pullback (the handle), with a buy trigger above the handle's high.
Currency intervention
Direct buying or selling of a currency by a government or central bank to influence its exchange rate.
Currency mismatch
A situation where debts are in one currency and income is in another, which magnifies the effect of a currency fall.
Currency peg
A policy of holding a currency at or near a fixed rate against another, such as the Hong Kong dollar's narrow band against the US dollar.
Currency risk
The risk that changes in exchange rates alter the value of foreign assets in your home currency.
Current yield
A bond's yearly coupon divided by its current price. It ignores any gain or loss at maturity.
Cushing
The town in Oklahoma where US oil futures are delivered, home to large storage tanks.
Custody
The safekeeping of securities on behalf of investors.
Customer concentration
The extent to which a company's sales depend on a few customers.
Cyclical sector
A sector whose profits rise and fall with the economy, such as consumer discretionary or industrials.

D

Daily loss limit
A preset maximum loss for one day. When it is reached, the trader stops until the next session.
Daily reset
A feature of leveraged and inverse products that restores their target multiple every day, making returns path-dependent.
Dark pool
A trading venue that does not display orders before they trade. Its trades are reported to the public tape afterwards.
Data snooping
Testing many ideas on the same data and reporting the best, which produces false discoveries.
Days inventory
Inventory divided by cost of goods sold, times 365: the average number of days stock is held.
Days payable outstanding (DPO)
Payables divided by the cost of purchases, times 365: the average number of days a company takes to pay suppliers.
Days sales outstanding (DSO)
Receivables divided by revenue, times 365: the average number of days customers take to pay.
Days to cover
Short interest divided by average daily volume: how many days of normal trading it would take for all short sellers to buy back their shares.
De-SPAC
The merger that takes a private company public through a SPAC.
Deal spread
The gap between a takeover offer and the target's current share price.
Death cross
The 50-day average crossing below the 200-day average.
Death spiral
A cycle in which share issuance and selling push the price down, which forces more issuance.
Debt avalanche
Repaying debts in order of interest rate, highest first, which minimises the total interest you pay.
Debt covenant
A condition in a loan agreement, such as a maximum ratio of net debt to EBITDA. Breaching it can let lenders demand repayment or change the terms.
Debt service ratio
The share of income that goes to paying interest and principal on debt.
Debt snowball
Repaying debts in order of balance, smallest first, to build momentum with quick wins.
Decentralised exchange (DEX)
A trading venue run by smart contracts, where users trade from their own wallets.
Decision journal
A dated record of why a decision was made, written before its result is known.
Declaration date
The date the board announces a dividend and its size.
Defensive sector
A sector whose demand is steady in any economy, such as consumer staples, health care and utilities.
Defined benefit (DB)
A pension where the employer promises a set income, usually based on your pay and years of service.
Defined contribution (DC)
A pension where your pot depends on what you and your employer pay in and how it is invested.
Defined-risk position
A position whose maximum possible loss is known and limited when it is opened.
Delayed quote
A price that is shown some minutes after it traded, as with many free data feeds.
Deleveraging
Cutting debt, often by selling assets. When many investors do it at once, it can push prices sharply lower.
Delta
How much an option's price changes for a $1 move in the stock. Times 100, it shows how many shares one contract behaves like.
Delta hedging
Trading the underlying asset to offset the delta of an options position, so the combined position is not sensitive to small price moves.
Denominator effect
The rise in the portfolio weight of slowly valued private assets after listed assets fall.
Depeg
When a stablecoin's price breaks away from its target, usually $1. A lasting depeg means the backing or trust has failed.
Depositary bank
The bank that issues depositary receipts and holds the underlying foreign shares in custody.
Derivative
A financial contract whose value depends on an underlying asset such as a share, an index, a currency or a commodity.
Devil's advocate
Arguing the opposite side on purpose to test the strength of an idea.
Dilution
A fall in each existing shareholder's ownership share when a company issues new shares.
Direct lending
Lending made directly to companies by non-bank funds rather than through banks or public bond markets.
Direct listing
A way to list existing shares on an exchange without a traditional underwritten offering.
Directional indicators (+DI and -DI)
Two lines showing how much of recent movement was upward (+DI) or downward (-DI). Whichever is higher shows the side in control of the trend.
Discount certificate
A certificate bought below the price of the underlying, with the gain capped at a set level.
Disposition effect
The tendency to sell winning investments too early and hold losing ones too long. It is closely linked to loss aversion and regret aversion.
Distribution waterfall
The order in which a private fund's proceeds are paid out to investors and the manager.
Distribution yield
The income a fund paid in the past year divided by its price, which says nothing about changes in the fund's value.
Dividend
A cash payment from a company's profits to shareholders.
Dividend aristocrat
An S&P 500 company that has increased its dividend every year for at least 25 years.
Dividend cover
How many times earnings or free cash flow cover the dividend; below one means the payout exceeds what the company earns.
Dividend cut
A company's decision to reduce or stop its dividend, often to protect cash.
Dividend per share (DPS)
The total dividend a company pays, divided by the number of shares, usually quoted for a year.
Dividend reinvestment plan (DRIP)
A plan that uses each dividend to buy more shares automatically.
Dividend yield
The yearly dividend per share divided by the share price, shown as a percentage.
Doji
A candle that opens and closes at almost the same price. It signals indecision between buyers and sellers.
Dollar index (DXY)
An index of the US dollar against six major currencies, with the euro making up more than half. A rising DXY means a stronger dollar.
Dollar volume
Price multiplied by the number of shares traded. Average daily dollar volume shows how easily a stock can be bought and sold.
Domicile
The country where a fund is legally based. It affects the taxes the fund pays, the rules it follows and who is allowed to buy it.
Donchian channel
A channel formed by the highest high and lowest low of the last N periods. A close above the upper line is a classic trend-following breakout signal.
Dot plot
A chart in which each FOMC member marks where they expect the policy rate to be in future years.
Double counting
Counting the same evidence twice because two factors measure nearly the same thing.
Downside deviation
The volatility of returns that fall below a minimum acceptable return.
Drawdown
A fall from a peak in account value or price, usually shown in %.
Drawdown lock
The point at which a trailing floor stops rising, often when it reaches the starting balance.
Drawdown rule
A pre-agreed action, such as halving size, taken when drawdown reaches a set level.
Dual mandate
The Federal Reserve's two goals set by law: maximum employment and stable prices.
Dual sourcing
Buying a key input from two or more suppliers to reduce dependence on one.
Duration
A measure of a bond's sensitivity to interest rates: the approximate percentage price change for a one point move in yield.
Dutch auction
A tender method in which holders name the lowest price they will accept within a range and the company sets one price that fills the offer.

E

Earnings blackout
A rule to avoid holding positions through a company's earnings announcement.
Earnings gap
A jump between the last close before a report and the first open after it, caused by results arriving outside regular trading hours.
Earnings per share (EPS)
Net income divided by the number of shares. Diluted EPS also counts options and convertibles that could become shares.
Earnings quality
How closely reported profit is backed by cash and how repeatable it is.
Earnings surprise
The difference between reported results and the consensus estimate, often shown as a percentage of the estimate.
EBIT
Earnings before interest and taxes, close to operating income. It measures the profit of the core business.
EBITDA
Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation. A rough measure of operating cash profit that ignores financing and spending on assets.
Economic calendar
A list of scheduled news, such as interest rate decisions, inflation and jobs figures and earnings dates, with the time each is due.
Economic moat
A lasting competitive advantage, such as a strong brand or a network effect, that protects a company's profits from rivals.
Economic profit
Operating profit after tax minus a charge for the capital used, showing whether a company earns more than it costs to fund.
EDGAR
The SEC's free online database of company filings.
Efficient frontier
The set of portfolios offering the highest expected return for each level of risk.
EMA
Exponential moving average: a moving average weighted towards recent prices.
Emergency fund
Cash set aside for unexpected costs or a loss of income, usually three to six months of essential spending.
Employer match
Money an employer adds to your pension in proportion to what you contribute.
Engulfing pattern
A two-candle pattern where the second candle's body completely covers the first candle's body, in the opposite direction. It suggests one side suddenly took control.
Enterprise value
The market value of a company's shares plus its debt minus its cash. Roughly the price of buying the whole business.
Enterprise value (EV)
Market capitalisation plus debt and similar claims, minus cash: the price of the whole operating business.
EPS
Earnings per share: a company's profit divided by its number of shares.
Equity curve
A chart of account value or cumulative R over the sequence of trades.
Equity offering
A sale of new shares by a company to raise money, which dilutes existing shareholders.
ESMA
The European Securities and Markets Authority, which coordinates securities regulation across the European Union.
Essential spending
The costs you must pay each month, such as housing, food, utilities, transport, insurance and minimum debt payments.
Estimate revision
A change by analysts to their forecast for a company. Revisions after a report show whether analysts think the news changes the future.
Estimative language
Vague words such as likely or probable that people read as very different probabilities.
ETF
Exchange-traded fund: a fund that trades like a stock, often tracking an index.
European option
An option that can be exercised only at expiry.
EV/EBITDA
Enterprise value divided by EBITDA. A multiple used to compare companies independently of their debt levels.
Evaluation (challenge)
A paid test account with a profit target and loss limits. Breaking any rule ends the attempt, and the fee is not returned.
Evergreen fund
An open-ended fund that continues indefinitely and offers periodic subscriptions and limited redemptions.
Ex-dividend date
The first day a share trades without the right to the next dividend, so new buyers do not receive it.
Ex-rights price
The theoretical share price after a rights issue, found by averaging old shares and new shares at their respective prices.
Exercise
The act of an option buyer using their right to buy (call) or sell (put) the underlying at the strike price.
Exhibit 99.1
The attachment to an 8-K that usually holds the earnings press release.
Exit scam
A crypto scam where the creators drain the trading pool or sell their holding, collapsing the price.
Exotic pair
A pair that combines a major currency with one from a smaller or emerging economy, such as USD/TRY. Spreads and swaps are usually larger.
Expectancy
The average amount you expect to win or lose per trade over many trades.
Expected cost
The probability of a loss multiplied by its size.
Expected move
The one-standard-deviation price range implied by volatility over a given period.
Expected shortfall
The average loss in the worst cases beyond a chosen threshold, such as the worst 5% of outcomes.
Expected value
The average result per trade over many trades: win probability times average win minus loss probability times average loss.
Export controls
Rules that restrict the sale of certain goods or technology to other countries.
Exposure map
A list of your holdings arranged by the risk channels that affect them.
Expropriation
A government's seizure of private assets, with little or no compensation.
Extended-hours trading
Trading before the regular open or after the regular close, usually with lower volume and wider spreads.

F

Face value (par)
The amount a bond's issuer repays at maturity, often $1,000 for a US company bond. Bond prices are quoted as a percentage of it.
Factor
A broad driver of returns shared by many assets, such as value, momentum or size.
Factor exposure
A portfolio's sensitivity to a shared driver of returns such as momentum, value, growth, size or interest rates. Positions with different tickers can share the same factor exposure.
Factor loading
How strongly a portfolio's returns respond to a particular factor.
Factor weight
The share of a composite score that a factor contributes, set by the model's designer.
Factoring
Selling receivables to a third party for immediate cash, sometimes with the risk of non-payment staying with the seller.
Fair value at issue
The market value of a structured product's components on the day it is issued, usually below the offer price.
Fallen angel
A bond cut from investment grade to high yield. Selling by funds that can only hold investment grade can push its price down.
False precision
Quoting a number with more accuracy than the evidence supports.
Fastest bear market
A fall of 20% or more that happens in an unusually short time, such as the 2020 Covid crash.
Fat tails
A pattern in which extreme returns occur more often than a normal distribution would predict.
Fat-finger error
An order entered with a typing mistake, such as an extra zero in the quantity.
FCA
The Financial Conduct Authority, the UK regulator of the conduct of financial firms and markets.
FCA Register
The Financial Conduct Authority's public list of authorised UK firms. Checking it, and using the contact details it shows, helps you avoid scams and clone firms.
FCF yield
Free cash flow divided by market capitalisation. It shows the cash generated per unit of price paid for the equity.
FDIC
The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, which insures US bank deposits up to a limit per depositor.
FFO
Funds from operations: a profit measure for REITs that adds back depreciation to net income.
FGDR
The Fonds de garantie des depots et de resolution, the French scheme that protects eligible bank deposits and securities, up to set limits, if a bank or investment firm fails.
Fiat money
Money issued by a government that is not backed by a commodity such as gold and works because people trust it.
Fibonacci retracement
A drawing tool that marks levels such as 38.2%, 50% and 61.8% of a prior swing. Traders use it to estimate how far a pullback might go.
Filtered sample
A set of examples, such as famous investors, from which the failures have dropped out of view.
Financial conditions index
A summary measure of how easy or tight it is to borrow, combining interest rates, spreads, share prices and the exchange rate.
Financial promotion
Any communication that invites or encourages someone to engage in a financial activity, such as an advert or an influencer post.
Financing level
The reference level of a turbo, equal to the borrowed part of the exposure, below which a long product is knocked out.
Fire sale
A forced sale of assets at depressed prices to raise cash quickly.
First notice day
The first day on which holders of a physically delivered futures contract can be notified of delivery.
Fiscal credibility
Investors' confidence that a government's tax and spending plans are sustainable.
Fiscal policy
Government decisions on taxation and spending that influence demand in the economy.
Fixed fractional sizing
Risking the same percentage of current account equity on every trade.
Fixed-term deposit
A savings deposit that pays a set rate in return for leaving the money untouched for an agreed period.
Flash crash
A very fast, deep fall in prices that mostly reverses within minutes, usually because buyers suddenly disappear.
Float
The number of a company's shares available for public trading. Low-float stocks can move very sharply on modest buying or selling.
Follow-on offering
A later sale of shares by a company that is already listed, often at a discount to the market price.
FOMC
The Federal Open Market Committee, the Fed body that sets US interest rate policy.
Fonds en euros
The part of an assurance-vie contract where the insurer generally guarantees the capital and credits interest each year.
Forced liquidation
A sale made not by choice but because of a margin call, a stop-loss, a risk limit or a fund redemption.
Forced seller
An owner who must sell at any price to meet debts or other obligations.
Foreign tax credit
A relief that offsets tax paid abroad against tax due at home on the same income.
Form 10-K
The annual report that US-listed companies file with the SEC, including audited financial statements.
Form 10-Q
The quarterly report that US-listed companies file with the SEC, with unaudited statements.
Form 1099-B
A US tax form from brokers that reports sale proceeds and, for most shares, cost basis.
Form 13F
A quarterly filing in which large US investment managers disclose their holdings, with a delay of up to 45 days.
Form 20-F
The annual report used by many foreign companies listed in the US.
Form 4
A filing by an insider reporting a change in holdings, due within two business days.
Form 8-K
A report of a specific significant event, due within four business days of its occurrence.
Forward guidance
What a central bank says about the likely future path of interest rates. It can move a currency as much as the rate decision itself.
Forward pricing
Pricing orders at the next valuation after they are placed, so the exact price is not known in advance.
Forward volatility
The volatility implied by option prices for the period between two future dates.
Fractal
A five-bar pattern in which the middle bar has a higher high, or a lower low, than the two bars on each side. It is a simple rule for marking swing points.
Fractional Kelly
Betting a set fraction of the Kelly criterion's size, often a quarter to a half. It gives up some growth for much smaller swings and some protection against overestimating the edge.
Free cash flow
Operating cash flow minus capital expenditure. The cash left to pay dividends, buy back shares, repay debt or make acquisitions.
Free cash flow (FCF)
Operating cash flow minus capital expenditure: the cash left over after the business pays to run and maintain its assets.
Free float
The part of a company's shares that outside investors can freely trade, excluding stakes held by governments, founders or strategic owners.
Front month
The futures contract with the nearest expiry date. It is usually the most traded contract and the one most charts show.
Front-running
Trading ahead of a client's order, using knowledge of it to profit from the expected price move.
Frontier market
A smaller, less liquid and harder-to-access market than an emerging market.
FSCS
The Financial Services Compensation Scheme, which can compensate eligible UK customers, up to a limit, if an authorised firm fails and their money or investments are missing.
Fund
A pool of investors' money used to buy a basket of investments, managed by a professional or an index rule.
Fund domicile
The country in which a fund is registered, which can affect the taxes it pays and the rules it follows.
Funded-trader programme
A private business, often called a prop firm, that sells evaluations of trading skill. Traders who pass trade a firm account and receive a share of its profits.
Funding rate
The periodic payment between long and short holders of a perpetual that keeps its price near the spot index.
Futures contract
A standardised, exchange-traded agreement to buy or sell an asset at a set price on a set future date.

G

Gambler's fallacy
The mistaken belief that an outcome is due after a run of the opposite result, when events are independent.
Gamma
How much delta changes for a $1 move in the stock. It is highest for at-the-money options near expiry.
Gap
A jump between one bar's close and the next bar's open.
Gap risk
The risk that a price opens far from the previous close, beyond a stop, so the loss is larger than planned.
Gapper
A stock trading well above or below its previous close before the open, usually because of news.
GARP
Growth at a reasonable price: seeking companies growing faster than average without paying a very high multiple.
Gas
The fee paid for computing work on Ethereum, priced in gwei, a billionth of an ETH.
GDP
Gross domestic product: the total value of goods and services produced in an economy over a period.
GDPR
The General Data Protection Regulation: European data protection law giving individuals rights over their personal data.
GDR
A global depositary receipt, similar to an ADR but traded on exchanges such as London or Luxembourg.
Gearing
The ratio of an asset's price to the cost of the warrant exposure to it, showing how leveraged the warrant is.
General partner (GP)
The manager of a private fund who makes investment decisions and earns fees and carried interest.
Geometric growth rate
The average compound growth per period, which accounts for the damage that losses do to later gains.
Geopolitical risk
The risk that wars, conflicts, sanctions or political changes disrupt economies and markets.
GICS
The Global Industry Classification Standard, which groups companies into eleven sectors, 25 industry groups, 74 industries and 163 sub-industries.
Gilt
A bond issued by the UK government. Index-linked gilts adjust their payments for inflation.
Glide path
A planned shift from growth assets towards safer assets as a goal such as retirement approaches.
Going concern
An accounting assumption that a company will keep operating for at least the next twelve months. A going concern warning from the auditor signals serious doubt.
Gold-silver ratio
The gold price divided by the silver price. It shows how many ounces of silver buy one ounce of gold.
Golden cross
The 50-day average crossing above the 200-day average.
Goodwill
The amount paid for an acquisition above the fair value of its net assets. It can be written down if the deal turns out worse than expected.
Greater fool theory
The idea of buying an overpriced asset because you expect someone else to pay even more. It works only while new buyers keep arriving.
Gross exposure
The value of long positions plus the value of short positions, ignoring their sign. It shows how much capital is in play, whatever the direction.
Gross margin
Gross profit divided by revenue. It shows how much of each sale is left after the direct cost of making the product.
Gross rental yield
Annual rent divided by the purchase price, before any costs.
Ground rent
A regular payment a leaseholder makes to the owner of the freehold.
Guaranteed return
A promised profit with no risk, which no honest investment can offer.
Guidance
A company's forecast for future results, often more important to the price than past results.
Guidance beat
When a company's forecast for future sales or profits comes in above what analysts expected.

H

H-shares
Shares of mainland Chinese companies listed in Hong Kong and priced in Hong Kong dollars.
Haircut
The percentage by which the value of collateral is reduced when it is used to secure a loan.
Half-life (mean reversion)
The time it takes for half of a deviation from the average to disappear.
Hallucination
A fluent statement from an AI model that is false or unsupported by any source.
Halving
The cut in half of bitcoin's block reward every 210,000 blocks, roughly every four years. It slows the creation of new coins.
Hammer
A candle with a small body near the top and a long lower shadow, appearing after a decline. It shows buyers rejected lower prices.
Hash
A short digital fingerprint calculated from data. Any change to the data produces a completely different hash.
Hawkish and dovish
Hawkish means leaning towards higher interest rates to fight inflation. Dovish means leaning towards lower rates to support growth.
Haystack idea
Bogle's advice to buy the whole market through a cheap index fund rather than search for the winning stock.
Health savings account (HSA)
A US account for medical costs with a tax deduction, tax-free growth and tax-free withdrawals for qualified expenses.
Heating degree day (HDD)
A measure of heating demand: 65 degrees Fahrenheit minus the day's average temperature, or zero if it is warmer. More HDDs mean colder weather and more gas use.
Hedge budget
A fixed share of a portfolio spent each year on protection, such as 1%.
Hedge ratio
The number of units of one asset to hold against another so that their combination is balanced, often the slope of a regression.
Hedged share class
A version of a fund that uses forward contracts to cancel most of the effect of currency moves on its holdings.
Hedger
A market participant who uses futures to reduce an existing price risk.
Held-to-maturity
An accounting category for bonds a bank intends to keep until repayment, so price falls are not shown in profit or loss.
Henry Hub
A natural gas pipeline hub in Louisiana. Its price is the main US natural gas benchmark and the basis for NYMEX gas futures.
Herding
Copying the actions of a crowd instead of relying on your own analysis. Widespread herding can push prices far from fair value and help inflate bubbles.
Hidden divergence
When price makes a higher low but the oscillator a lower low (bullish), or price a lower high but the oscillator a higher high (bearish). It hints the trend will continue.
High-water mark
The highest value an account has reached so far. Drawdowns are measured from it.
High-yield bond
A bond rated below investment grade, also called junk. It pays a higher yield because its issuer is more likely to default.
Historical volatility
The annualised standard deviation of an asset's past returns, measuring how much it actually moved.
Hit rate
The share of signals or trades that turn out correct or profitable.
Holding-period test
The requirement to hold shares for more than 60 days around the ex-dividend date for a dividend to be taxed at lower rates.
Home bias
The tendency to hold far more of your own country's assets than its share of the world market would suggest.
House money effect
The tendency to take bigger risks with recent gains, as if they were not really your own money.
Hurdle rate
The minimum return, such as 8% a year, that investors must receive before a fund manager earns carried interest.
Hysteresis
Using different thresholds to switch into and out of a state so that a signal does not flip repeatedly near one level.
Hysteresis band
Using different thresholds to start and to stop a rule, so it does not flip back and forth around a single level.

I

Iceberg order
A large order that displays only a small part of its size and refills as that part is filled.
Ichimoku Cloud
A Japanese charting method built from midpoints of the 9, 26 and 52-bar ranges. The cloud between its two leading spans, plotted 26 bars ahead, marks the main trend and support zone.
Idea funnel
The process of narrowing thousands of assets to a few trades through successive filters and reviews.
IFU
The imprime fiscal unique, the yearly tax summary a French bank or broker sends, listing dividends, interest and sales. It pre-fills much of the French tax return.
Illiquidity premium
The extra return investors require for holding an asset that cannot be sold quickly.
Impact cost
The extra cost of an order from moving the price, on top of the bid-ask spread.
Impermanent loss
The shortfall a liquidity provider suffers compared with simply holding the tokens, caused by prices moving apart. It becomes real when you withdraw.
Implementation shortfall
The gap between the price when you decided to trade and your final result, including fees and the cost of shares left unfilled.
Implied move
The size of move, up or down, that option prices suggest for an event such as earnings. It is often estimated from the at-the-money straddle.
Implied volatility
The volatility that makes a pricing model match an option's market price. It is the market's forecast of future movement, quoted as a yearly percentage.
Impulse wave
In Elliott wave theory, a five-wave move in the direction of the main trend. It is followed by a three-wave correction.
Inclusion factor
A scaling applied by index providers to a company's weight to reflect how much of it foreign investors can actually buy.
Income drawdown
Taking income from invested pension money while leaving the rest invested.
Income floor
Guaranteed income, such as pensions and annuities, that covers essential spending.
Income portfolio
A portfolio built mainly to produce regular cash flow, such as dividends and interest.
Income protection
Insurance that pays a share of your income if illness or injury stops you from working.
Income units
Fund units that pay out dividends and interest as cash.
Index
A basket of assets tracked as one number, like the S&P 500 or FTSE 100.
Index fund
A fund that holds the companies in a market index to match its return, usually at a low cost.
Indicative NAV (iNAV)
A live estimate of an ETF's net asset value per share, updated through the trading day so traders can judge a fair price.
Indicator redundancy
When indicators built from the same inputs mostly give the same signal, so their agreement adds little evidence. Statisticians call the general problem multicollinearity.
Industry group
A level of classification between sector and industry that groups related businesses.
Inelastic demand
Demand that changes very little when prices change, as with oil in the short run.
Inflation
The general rise in prices over time, which reduces what a given amount of money can buy.
Information ratio
Active return divided by tracking error: extra return per unit of benchmark-relative risk.
Initial margin
The deposit required to open a futures position.
Inside bar
A candle whose high and low both sit within the previous candle's range, called the mother bar. It shows the market contracting before a possible breakout.
Inside view
Forecasting from the specific details of the case in front of you.
Insider dealing
Trading on specific, non-public information that would likely move a price if it were public.
Insider ownership
The share of a company held by its directors and senior managers.
Insider transaction
A purchase or sale of a company's shares by its directors or senior executives, which must be reported.
Insurance excess
The part of a claim you pay yourself before the insurer pays the rest, also called a deductible.
Insurance fund
A reserve built from liquidation surpluses that covers losses when a liquidated position cannot be closed at its bankruptcy price.
Interbank market
The network of large banks that trade currencies directly with each other and supply prices to brokers and clients.
Interest rate differential
The gap between the interest rates of two currencies. Changes in its expected path are a major driver of exchange rates.
Interest-only mortgage
A loan on which you pay only the interest, leaving the full amount to be repaid at the end.
Internal rate of return (IRR)
The annual rate of return that makes the present value of an investment's cash flows equal to zero.
Internalisation
When a firm fills customer orders from its own inventory instead of sending them to an exchange.
Intraday margin deficit
A shortfall that opens up during the trading day when a purchase or short sale leaves a margin account's equity below the margin it must hold. FINRA's 2026 rule requires it to be cured promptly.
Intrinsic value
An estimate of what a business is really worth, based on the cash it can produce over its life. It can differ a lot from the market price.
Inverse contract
A derivative margined and settled in the underlying coin, so profit and loss are paid in that coin.
Inverse volatility weighting
Setting each position's weight in proportion to 1 divided by its volatility. Calmer assets get bigger weights, so each position carries a similar standalone swing.
Inverted yield curve
When short-term yields are higher than long-term yields. In the US it has often come before recessions, with timing that varies widely.
Investing style
A repeatable rule for choosing shares based on a characteristic such as cheapness, growth, quality, price trend or size.
Investment grade
A credit rating of BBB- or higher at S&P and Fitch, or Baa3 or higher at Moody's. It signals a lower risk of default.
Investment trust
A listed company that holds investments and issues a fixed number of shares traded on an exchange.
IPO
Initial public offering: a company's first sale of shares to the public.
Iron condor
A defined-risk strategy that combines an out-of-the-money put credit spread and call credit spread. It profits if the stock stays between the short strikes.
Isolated margin
A mode in which only the margin assigned to one position can be lost if it is liquidated.
Issuer credit risk
The risk that the bank issuing a note fails and cannot pay what it owes.
Issuer margin
The difference between the price of a structured product and the fair value of its components, kept by the issuer.
IV crush
A sharp fall in implied volatility after a known event such as earnings. It cuts option prices even when the stock moves.
IV rank
A measure of where current implied volatility sits within its range over a past period, such as one year.

J

J-curve
The typical pattern in private funds of negative early returns, caused by fees and unrealised investments, followed by a rise.
Journal mining
Searching a trade log for a rule that would have worked, which tends to find noise rather than a real edge.
Just-in-time inventory
A system that keeps minimal stock and relies on exactly timed deliveries.

K

Kelly criterion
A formula for the fraction of capital to risk that maximizes long-run growth when the edge is known exactly: W - (1 - W) / R. Traders usually use a fraction of it because real edges are uncertain.
Kelly fraction
The share of wealth to bet that maximises long-run growth for a given edge, which is 2p - 1 for an even-money bet.
Keltner channel
Bands set a multiple of ATR above and below an EMA, often 2 ATRs around a 20-period EMA. They are smoother than Bollinger Bands.
Key audit matter
An area of the accounts that the auditor found most significant or difficult, described in the audit report.
Key information document
A short standard document that explains a fund's aims, risks, costs and past performance.
Key information document (KID)
A standard short document that sets out the risks, costs and scenarios of a packaged investment product.
Kill switch
A control that halts automated trading when limits are breached or something fails.
Knock-in barrier
A level that, if breached, exposes the investor to the underlying's losses at maturity.
Knowledge cutoff
The date after which an AI model has no training data and so does not know recent events.
KYC
Know Your Customer: the process by which a firm verifies a client's identity and background.

L

Lagging indicator
An indicator that confirms a move after it has started, such as a moving average or ADX. Smoothing removes noise at the cost of later signals.
Large language model
An AI system trained on large amounts of text that generates replies by predicting likely continuations.
Latency arbitrage
Profiting from being faster than others, for example by trading against quotes on a venue that has not yet updated to a new price.
Launch-day sell-off
Selling on the day a long-awaited product or event arrives, as traders take profits.
Layering
A form of spoofing that uses several orders at different price levels to create a false impression of depth.
Leaderboard
A ranking of traders by results, which tends to favour the riskiest and luckiest.
Leading indicator
An indicator that tends to turn before price does, usually a fast oscillator. It reacts early because of its short window, not because it predicts, so it gives more false signals.
Leak
A repeated small loss of edge from costs, slippage or rule breaks that is not part of the strategy.
Lender of last resort
A central bank that lends to solvent but illiquid banks to stop a panic.
Level 2
A view of the order book showing bids and asks waiting at several prices, not just the best ones. It does not show hidden or off-exchange orders.
Leverage
Using borrowed money to take a bigger position. Magnifies gains and losses.
Leverage effect
The tendency for equity volatility to rise when prices fall and to fall when prices rise.
Leveraged buyout (LBO)
The purchase of a company using a large amount of borrowed money, secured on the company's own assets and cash flow.
Leveraged token
A token that rebalances daily to keep a fixed multiple of an asset's daily return, which causes decay in choppy markets.
Likelihood ratio
How many times more likely the evidence is if an idea is true than if it is false.
Limit order
An order to trade only at a set price or better.
Limited partner (LP)
An investor in a private fund who supplies capital but does not manage it.
Linear contract
A derivative margined and settled in a stablecoin, so profit and loss are simple dollar amounts.
Liquidation cascade
A chain reaction in which forced sales push the price down and trigger further liquidations.
Liquidation price
The price at which an exchange closes a leveraged position because the margin is nearly used up.
Liquidity
How easily an asset trades without moving the price. High liquidity means tight spreads.
Liquidity buffer
Cash and easily sold assets held so that you are never forced to sell at the bottom of a shock.
Liquidity coverage ratio (LCR)
A rule requiring banks to hold enough high-quality liquid assets to cover 30 days of stressed outflows.
Liquidity filter
A screen condition, such as minimum average dollar volume, that removes assets that are hard to trade.
Liquidity limit
A cap on position size based on how much you could sell in a few days without dominating the volume.
Liquidity risk
The risk of being unable to trade a position quickly without a large price impact.
Live account
A trading account funded with real money, where every gain and loss is real.
Live record
A track record built from signals stored with timestamps before their outcomes were known.
Lock-up
A period after an IPO when insiders and early investors agree not to sell their shares.
Long
Owning an asset to profit if it rises.
Long-term capital gain
A profit on an asset held for more than one year, taxed at lower rates than ordinary income.
Longevity risk
The risk of living longer than your savings can support.
Look-ahead bias
A backtest error where a decision uses information that was not yet available at the time, such as a closing price before the close. It makes results look better than anything achievable.
Losing streak
A run of consecutive losing trades. Streaks appear by chance even in profitable strategies.
Loss aversion
The tendency for a loss to feel more painful than an equal gain feels good, roughly twice as strong in many experiments. It makes people reluctant to accept and realise losses.
Lost decade
A long stretch of weak growth and flat or falling asset prices. The term is widely used for Japan after its 1980s bubble burst.
Lot
A standard forex trade size. A standard lot is 100,000 units of the base currency, a mini lot 10,000 and a micro lot 1,000.
Low-volatility anomaly
The finding that less volatile shares have earned similar or higher returns than very volatile ones.

M

M2
A broad measure of the money supply that includes cash, checking and savings deposits and similar assets.
MACD
Moving Average Convergence Divergence: the gap between the 12 and 26-period EMAs, with a 9-period signal line.
Maintenance margin
The minimum equity needed to keep a position open. Falling below it triggers a margin call or forced closing.
Major pair
One of the most traded currency pairs, all of which include the US dollar, such as EUR/USD, USD/JPY and GBP/USD.
Maker fee
The fee charged on an order that adds liquidity to the order book, usually lower than the taker fee.
Maker-taker model
An exchange fee model that pays a small rebate to orders that add liquidity and charges orders that remove it.
Mandatory corporate action
A corporate event such as a split that happens to your holding without any choice from you.
MAR ratio
Compound annual growth rate (CAGR) divided by maximum drawdown. It shows how much growth a system delivered for each unit of its worst fall.
Margin call
A broker's demand for more cash when falling prices shrink the investor's own stake. If it is not met, the broker can sell the position.
Margin call trigger
The account value below which a broker demands more cash or sells positions, found as the loan divided by one minus the maintenance requirement.
Margin of error
The range of uncertainty around an average result, roughly two standard errors either side.
Margin of safety
The gap between your estimate of a share's value and the price you pay. A bigger gap leaves more room for errors.
Mark price
A fair-value price, usually based on an index of spot prices, used to calculate unrealised profit and trigger liquidations.
Mark to market
The daily revaluation of a position to the settlement price, with gains and losses settled in cash.
Market abuse

Having the right but not the obligation to benefit from an upside while limiting the cost of being wrong.

The value of confirmed orders a company has not yet delivered, a guide to future revenue.

The list of all resting limit orders to buy (bids) and sell (offers) at each price level.

A test of a rule on data that was not used to discover it.

A daily charge for holding a leveraged position past the end of the trading day.

A reading (e.g. RSI below 30) showing price has fallen fast and is stretched.
Oversubscribed
Describes an offering where investors ask for more shares than are available.

Holding a larger share of a sector or asset than the benchmark does.

Moving money into savings automatically on payday, before you have the chance to spend it.

A number from 0 to 1 measuring how likely an event is.

Risk-off
A market mood in which investors sell risky assets and move to safe havens.
Risk-on
A market mood in which investors seek returns and buy risky assets.
Risk-on and risk-off
Market moods. Risk-on favours shares and higher-yielding currencies; risk-off favours safe havens such as the yen, the Swiss franc and the dollar.
Risk-weighted assets (RWA)
A bank's assets, each multiplied by a weight reflecting its risk, used to set capital requirements.
Robo-adviser
An automated service that builds and manages a portfolio from a questionnaire about your goals and risk comfort.
ROIC
Return on invested capital: NOPAT divided by the capital invested in the business. It measures how efficiently operations turn capital into profit.
Role reversal
When broken resistance later acts as support, or broken support acts as resistance. Also called polarity.
Roll
Closing a position in a near-expiry futures contract and opening it in a later one to keep the exposure.
Roll decay
The steady loss in value of a product that must keep selling cheaper expiring futures and buying dearer later ones in contango.
Roll yield
The gain or loss from replacing an expiring futures contract with a later one. It is negative in contango and positive in backwardation.
Rolling correlation
Correlation recalculated over a moving window, such as the last 60 trading days. It shows how the relationship between two assets changes over time.
Rolling expectancy
Average result per trade calculated over a moving window of recent trades.
Roth conversion
Moving money from a traditional retirement account to a Roth account and paying income tax on the amount converted.
Roth IRA
An individual retirement account funded with after-tax money, in which qualified withdrawals are tax-free.
Roth-style account
An account funded with after-tax money, in which growth and qualified withdrawals are tax-free.
Round-trip cost
The total cost of opening and closing a position, including spread, commissions and slippage.
Round-tripping
Sending money out and bringing it back as revenue, with no real economic substance.
RS line
A stock's price divided by an index's price, plotted over time. A rising line means the stock is outperforming the index.
RSI
Relative Strength Index: momentum on a 0 to 100 scale.
Rug pull
A scam where a project's creators take investors' money or dump their tokens and abandon the project.
Ruin point
The market move at which a leveraged or inverse position loses all of its value.
Rule 10b5-1 plan
A pre-arranged written plan that lets insiders sell or buy shares on a set schedule.
Rule of 72
A shortcut for doubling time: divide 72 by the yearly return. At 8% a year, money doubles in about 9 years.
Rule-based screen
A screen with fixed, written criteria that are set before looking at the results.

S

Safe harbour
A rule under which you avoid a penalty if you meet a stated condition, such as paying a set share of last year's tax in advance.
Safe haven
An asset investors buy for safety in turbulent times, such as US Treasuries, the dollar, the yen, the Swiss franc or gold.
Safe withdrawal rate
The share of a portfolio you can take out each year with a low chance of running out. The 4% rule is the best-known estimate.
Same-store sales
Sales growth at stores that were open in both periods, used to judge a retailer without the effect of new openings.
Sanctions
Government measures such as asset freezes and trade bans, used to pressure a country, firm or person.
Say on pay
A shareholder vote on executive compensation, which is advisory in most markets.
Scaling plan
A firm's scheme for increasing a funded trader's account size after a period of consistent profits.
Scalping
A very short-term style that aims to capture small price moves many times a day. Costs matter enormously because each win is small.
Scenario portfolio test
Estimating a portfolio's return under several assumed shocks to find its weak points.
Scenario weighting
Assigning probabilities to bull, base and bear cases and combining them into one expected result.
Schedule 13D
A filing by a holder of more than 5% of a company's shares who may seek to influence it.
Screen drift
The gradual change in a screen's results as its rules are tweaked over time, which makes comparison impossible.
Screener
A tool that filters a large universe of assets using rules you set, such as size, growth, trend or volume.
Screening universe
The starting list of assets a screener filters, such as an index or all stocks on an exchange.
Scrip dividend
A dividend paid in extra shares instead of cash.
Scuttlebutt
Philip Fisher's method of learning about a company from customers, suppliers, competitors and former employees.
SEC
The US Securities and Exchange Commission, which regulates securities markets and protects investors.
Second-level thinking
Howard Marks's term for asking what is already priced in and what others are missing, rather than accepting the obvious view.
Secondary market
The market where existing securities are traded between investors, with no money going to the issuer.
Secondary test
In the Wyckoff method, a return toward a climax low after the first bounce. Lighter volume on the test suggests selling has dried up.
Section 104 holding
The UK pool of your shares of one class in one company, held at their average cost. Sales not matched with same-day or 30-day purchases are taken from this pool.
Section 1256 contract
A regulated futures or broad-based index option contract taxed 60% as long-term and 40% as short-term.
Sector
A broad group of companies that share a similar business and economic drivers, such as health care or financials.
Sector ETF
An exchange-traded fund that holds the companies of a single sector.
Sector limit
A cap on how much of a portfolio can be held in one sector.
Sector rotation
Money shifting from one group of industries to another as the economy and interest rates change.
Securities lending
Lending a fund's holdings to other firms for a fee, against collateral. The income can offset costs but adds a small risk.
Seed phrase
A list of 12 or 24 words that backs up a wallet's private keys. Anyone who has it controls the funds.
Seed phrase theft
A scam that tricks you into typing your wallet's 12 or 24 recovery words into a fake site or app.
Segmentation
Splitting trades into groups, such as by setup or time of day, to compare their results.
Sell the news
A pattern in which a stock falls after good news that the market had already priced in.
Sensitivity analysis
Changing one input at a time, such as the discount rate, to see how much a valuation changes.
Sequence-of-returns risk
The danger that poor returns early in retirement, while you are withdrawing money, permanently shrink how long your pot lasts.
Service charge
A fee paid by a leaseholder to cover the upkeep of shared parts of a building.
Session overlap
The hours when two major trading centres are open at once, especially London and New York. It is usually the most liquid part of the forex day.
Settlement
The exchange of cash for securities that completes a trade, usually one or two business days after the trade date.
Setup tag
A label that records which pattern or rule a trade was taken under, so results can be grouped.
Shadow banking
Credit provided by non-bank institutions that perform bank-like functions without a bank's safety net.
Shareholder yield
The dividend yield plus the net buyback yield, which counts shares issued as a negative.
Sharpe ratio
Return above the risk-free rate divided by the standard deviation of returns, a measure of return per unit of volatility. The Sortino ratio is similar but counts only downside volatility.
Shock playbook
A written sequence of steps to follow when a sudden event hits markets: pause, map exposure, stress and decide.
Short
Selling borrowed assets to profit if the price falls. Losses are theoretically unlimited.
Short interest
The total number of shares that have been sold short and not yet bought back, often expressed as a percentage of the float.
Short squeeze
A sharp rise as short sellers are forced to buy back.
Short-term capital gain
A profit on an asset held for one year or less, taxed as ordinary income.
Signal
A summary of evidence that points toward a direction over a stated horizon, produced by a rule, a model or a person.
Signal (news)
Information that can materially change the expected future cash flows or risk of an asset.
Signal horizon
The time frame, such as intraday, swing or position, for which a signal was designed.
Signal service
A provider that sends trade alerts which you act on yourself.
SIM swap
A fraud where a criminal moves your phone number to their SIM to intercept text codes.
Simple interest
Interest calculated only on the original amount, with no interest on past interest.
Simulated funded account
A funded account whose trades never reach a real market. Payouts are paid from the firm's own funds.
Simulated trading
Practising trades with virtual money in a model of the market, without real risk.
Sinking fund
Money set aside regularly to pay for large future repairs or replacements.
SIPC
The Securities Investor Protection Corporation, which protects customers of a failed US broker against missing assets.
Size premium
The extra return small companies have historically earned compared with large ones.
Skewness
A measure of the asymmetry of a distribution. Equity returns usually have negative skew: large falls are bigger than large rises.
Skip rule
A condition written in advance under which you will not take a trade even if a signal appears.
Slippage
The difference between expected and actual fill price.
SMA
Simple moving average: the plain average of the last N closes.
Smart contract
A program stored on a blockchain that runs automatically when its conditions are met.
Smart order router
Broker software that decides where to send each order, weighing price, size, the chance of a fill, speed and fees.
Social engineering
Manipulating people, rather than breaking software, to get access or money.
Social trading
Trading on a platform where you can see, follow and copy other traders' activity.
Soft landing
A slowdown that brings inflation down without causing a recession.
Sortino ratio
Excess return divided by downside deviation, counting only volatility below a target.
SPAC
A special purpose acquisition company, a shell that raises cash in an IPO to merge with a private business.
Special dividend
A one-off dividend paid in addition to a company's regular payments.
Special opening quotation (SOQ)
The special calculation of the VIX from opening option prices on a Wednesday, used to settle VIX futures and options.
Special purpose entity (SPE)
A separate legal entity set up for a narrow purpose, sometimes used to hold assets and debt away from the parent.
Speculative bubble
A period when prices rise far above any sensible estimate of value, driven mainly by the hope of selling to someone else for more. Bubbles usually end with a sharp fall.
Spin-off
The separation of a division into a new listed company, with shares given to the parent's holders. Also called a demerger.
Sponsor promote
The founder shares, often 20% of a SPAC, that sponsors receive for a nominal price.
Spoofing
Placing orders with the intention of cancelling them before they trade, to mislead others about supply or demand.
Spot bitcoin ETF
An exchange-traded fund that holds actual bitcoin, letting investors own it through a normal brokerage account.
Spot price
The price for buying or selling a commodity for immediate delivery. Futures prices converge towards it as they near expiry.
Spread
The gap between the bid and the ask.
Spread betting
A UK and Irish product where you stake an amount per point of price movement. Under current UK rules, profits are generally free of capital gains tax.
Square-root-of-time rule
The rule that volatility scales with the square root of time, so daily volatility is annual volatility divided by about 15.9.
Staged scaling
Increasing trading size in steps, moving up only after meeting set tests.
Stagflation
A period of high inflation combined with weak growth and high unemployment.
Stale signal
A signal based on data old enough that the market may have moved and the plan no longer applies.
Stalwart
A large, established company with steady, moderate growth, in Peter Lynch's classification.
Standard error
The uncertainty of an estimate such as a mean. For a mean it is the standard deviation divided by the square root of the sample size.
Standardised unexpected earnings (SUE)
An earnings surprise divided by the standard deviation of past surprises. It shows how unusual a surprise is for that company.
State pension
A public pension paid by the government, usually funded by current workers' taxes and based on your contribution record.
Static drawdown
A loss floor fixed below the starting balance that never moves, so profits widen the cushion above it.
Stationarity
The property of a series whose mean, variance and relationships remain stable over time.
Statistical arbitrage
A market-neutral approach that trades many small, short-lived mispricings across a large set of securities using models.
Step-up in basis
The reset of an inherited asset's cost basis to its market value at the owner's death.
Stochastic oscillator
A 0 to 100 reading of where the close sits within the high to low range of the last N bars. %K is the main line and %D is its 3-bar average.
Stock dividend
A distribution of extra shares to existing holders in proportion to what they own, such as one new share for every ten held.
Stock split
A change that increases the number of shares and reduces the price per share in proportion, leaving the company's value unchanged.
Stocks-to-use ratio
A crop's stocks at the end of the season divided by a year's total use. A low ratio means a thin buffer and prices that react strongly to bad news.
Stop run
A brief move through an obvious level that triggers the stop orders clustered there, often followed by a reversal. Also called a liquidity sweep.
Stop-copy limit
A set loss level at which your copy of a trader is closed automatically.
Stop-limit order
An order that becomes a limit order when a trigger price is reached, protecting the price but possibly not filling.
Stop-loss
An order that exits a losing trade at a set price.
Stop-out level
The margin level at which a broker starts closing your positions automatically to stop further losses.
Stop-trading rule
A pre-set trigger, such as a total loss limit, at which you pause or stop live trading.
Stopping rule
A rule for when to stop looking and choose, such as observing the first 37% of options before picking.
Straddle
A long call and long put with the same strike and expiry, profiting from a large move in either direction.
Strangle
A long out-of-the-money call and put with different strikes, profiting from a large move at a lower cost than a straddle.
Strategic allocation
The long-term target mix of asset classes, based on goals and risk tolerance.
Stress test
An estimate of how much the current portfolio would lose in a specific severe scenario, either historical or invented. It focuses on rare events that everyday risk measures miss.
Strike price
The fixed price at which an option lets its holder buy (call) or sell (put) the underlying stock.
Structured product
A package, usually a bank note combined with derivatives, designed to deliver a specific payoff.
Style box
A grid that classifies funds by company size and by value, blend or growth orientation.
Style drift
A change in a fund's holdings that moves it away from the style it claims.
Subprime mortgage
A home loan made to a borrower with a weak credit record. Defaults on these loans helped trigger the 2008 crisis.
Sudden stop
An abrupt halt in foreign capital flowing into a country.
Suitability
The requirement for advisers to recommend products that fit a client's goals, finances, knowledge and tolerance for loss.
Sum of the parts (SOTP)
A valuation that values each division of a company separately with suitable multiples, then adds the results.
Summary risk indicator
A scale from 1 to 7 in a KID that shows a product's overall level of risk.
Sunk cost fallacy
Letting money or effort already spent, which cannot be recovered, influence decisions about the future.
Supertrend
A trailing stop line set a multiple of ATR away from the bar's midpoint. It only moves in the trend's direction and flips when price closes through it.
Supplier finance
An arrangement in which a bank pays a company's suppliers early while the company pays the bank later.
Supply shock
A sudden fall in the availability of a key good, which raises its price and hurts growth.
Support
A price area where buying has previously stopped declines.
Survivorship bias
Testing only on assets that still exist today, ignoring those that went bankrupt or were delisted. It usually makes past results look too good.
Swap (rollover)
The daily interest credit or charge for holding a leveraged forex or CFD position overnight, based on the interest rate differential plus a broker markup.
Swing point
A swing high is a peak with lower highs on each side; a swing low is a trough with higher lows on each side. Each is confirmed only after price moves away from it.
Swing trade
A trade held for a few days to a few weeks to capture one move, or swing, within a larger trend.
Swing trading
Holding trades for days to weeks to capture a move.
Switching costs
The time, money or effort a customer would lose by changing supplier, which helps protect a company's sales.
Sycophancy
The tendency of an AI model to agree with the user instead of giving an independent answer.
Sympathy move
A price move in one stock caused by news from a related company rather than by its own results.
Synergies
Cost savings or extra revenue expected from combining two companies.
Synthetic replication
Delivering an index's return through a swap contract with a bank rather than by holding the index's assets. It adds counterparty risk.

T

t-statistic
An estimate divided by its standard error, used to judge whether it is different from zero. Values above about 2 are usually considered significant.
Tactical allocation
Small, temporary deviations from the strategic allocation based on shorter-term views.
Tail hedge
A position, usually out-of-the-money puts, that pays off in a severe market fall.
Tail risk
The risk of rare, extreme events far outside the normal range of outcomes.
Takeover premium
The amount by which an offer price exceeds the target's market price before the bid.
Taker fee
The fee charged on an order that removes liquidity from the order book by matching an existing order.
Taper tantrum
The sell-off in emerging markets in 2013 after the US central bank signalled it would slow its bond purchases.
Target-date fund
A fund that follows a glide path automatically for a chosen retirement year.
Tariff
A tax charged on imported goods.
Tax treaty
An agreement between two countries that reduces the tax charged on income, such as dividends, paid across their border.
Tax wrapper
An account, such as an ISA, a PEA or an IRA, that shields investments from some taxes, usually within yearly limits.
Tax-deferred account
An account where contributions reduce your taxable income now and you pay tax when you withdraw.
Tax-loss harvesting
Selling investments at a loss to offset gains or income while staying invested through a similar holding.
Taxable equivalent yield
The yield a taxable bond must pay to match the after-tax yield of a tax-free bond.
Ten-bagger
An investment that rises to ten times its purchase price.
Tender offer
An offer to buy shares directly from holders, often at a premium and for a limited time.
Term structure (volatility)
The pattern of implied volatility across different expiry dates.
Terminal value
In a DCF, the value of all cash flows beyond the forecast period, usually estimated with a steady long-term growth rate.
Theta
How much an option's price falls per day from the passing of time, with everything else unchanged.
Thin float
A small number of shares available for public trading, which makes the price easier to move.
Tick size
The smallest price movement a futures contract can make.
Tick value
The dollar value of one tick for one contract.
Tick volume
The number of price changes in a period. It is used as a stand-in for volume in markets without central volume data, such as spot forex.
Tilt
An emotional state after losses in which a trader abandons rules and takes impulsive trades.
Time and sales
Also called the tape: a live list of executed trades with their price, size and time.
Time horizon
The length of time until you need the money, which decides how much risk it can safely take.
Time stop
An exit rule that closes a trade if it has not moved as expected within a set number of sessions.
Time value
The part of an option's price above its intrinsic value. Also called extrinsic value, it shrinks to zero by expiry.
Time-series momentum
A strategy that holds an asset only while its own trend is up, rather than ranking it against others.
TIPS
Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities: US government bonds whose principal rises with inflation, so their yield is a real yield.
Total return
The change in an investment's price plus all income received, such as dividends and interest.
Total return approach
Funding your spending from a mix of income and selling small parts of the portfolio, rather than relying on dividends only.
Toxic convertible
A convertible note whose conversion price floats at a discount to recent market prices, so lower prices create more shares.
Tracking difference
A fund's return minus its index's return over a period. It shows what running the fund really cost, beyond the headline fee.
Tracking error
How much the gap between a fund's return and its index's return varies over time. Low tracking error means the fund follows its index steadily.
Trade balance
Exports minus imports. A negative balance is a trade deficit.
Trade log
A table with one row per trade recording the plan, the outcome and notes, used to analyse your results.
Trade record
A written record of each trade, including date, asset, size, prices, fees and the reason for it.
Trading restriction
A limit a broker places on buying or selling a stock, often because of extreme volatility or funding needs.
Trading strategy
A complete set of rules covering setup, entry, position size, stop, target, exit and the evidence for its expected value.
Trading suspension
A temporary halt in trading of a security ordered by an exchange or regulator.
Traditional IRA
An individual retirement account in which contributions may be tax-deductible and withdrawals are taxed as income.
Trailing drawdown
A loss floor that follows the account's highest balance or equity by a set amount. Profits move the floor up instead of widening the cushion.
Trailing stop
A stop that follows price by a fixed distance as the trade moves in your favour.
Transmission channel
A route, such as commodity prices or supply chains, through which an event affects asset prices.
Treasury bill
US government debt that matures in a year or less. It pays no coupon: you buy it below face value and receive face value at maturity.
Treaty rate
The reduced withholding rate that applies to dividends paid between two countries that have a tax treaty.
Trend
The general direction of price: higher highs and lows (up) or lower highs and lows (down).
Trend following
A strategy that buys rising assets and sells falling ones, holding until the trend ends. It typically wins less than half its trades but captures large gains.
Treynor ratio
Excess return over the risk-free rate divided by beta: return per unit of market risk.
Trigger price
The price at which a planned setup becomes a trade, often with an alert set at that level.
Trillion-dollar company
A company whose total market value is above one trillion dollars.
Turbo certificate
A leveraged product with a financing level that ends the product if the underlying touches it.
Turnaround
A troubled company expected to recover, in Peter Lynch's classification.
Turtles
The group of novice traders trained by Richard Dennis and William Eckhardt in the 1980s to follow a trend-following system.
Two-factor authentication
A login that needs a second proof, such as a code from an app, as well as your password.

U

UCITS
The EU's standard rulebook for investment funds sold to the public, covering diversification, liquidity and investor protection. Most European ETFs are UCITS funds.
Underweight
Holding a smaller share of a sector or asset than the benchmark does.
Undisturbed price
The share price before a takeover bid or rumour became known, used to measure the premium offered.
Uninsured deposits
Deposits above the limit covered by deposit insurance, which are at risk if the bank fails.
Unit
A share of a fund, priced at its net asset value per unit.
Unit trust
An open-ended UK fund set up under a trust, where investors hold units.
Unites de compte
Unit-linked investments inside an assurance-vie contract, such as share funds or ETFs. Their value can rise or fall, and the capital is not guaranteed.
Unlawful disclosure
Passing inside information to another person other than in the normal course of work.
Upside participation
The share of an index's gain that a structured product passes on to the investor.
Useful life
The period over which an asset is expected to be used, which sets how fast it is depreciated.

V

Valuation spread
The gap between how cheaply the cheapest and the most expensive shares are valued.
Value area
The band of prices around the point of control that holds about 70% of a profile's volume. Its edges are the value area high and value area low.
Value at Risk (VaR)
A loss threshold that should not be exceeded at a chosen confidence level over a set period, if the model is right. A 95% one-day VaR of $2,000 means about 19 days in 20 should lose less than that.
Value of information
The improvement in expected result from learning something before making a decision.
Value trap
A stock that looks cheap on ratios because its business is deteriorating, so it stays cheap or falls further.
Variable interest entity (VIE)
A structure in which a listed offshore company controls a domestic operating business through contracts rather than direct ownership.
Variance
The average squared deviation from the mean, measuring how widely values are spread.
Variance (luck)
The normal run of wins and losses around a strategy's average, including losing streaks that do not mean the edge has gone.
Variance premium
The amount by which implied variance has on average exceeded the variance that followed, earned by sellers of volatility.
Variance swap
A contract that pays the difference between realised variance and a fixed strike variance.
Vega
How much an option's price changes when implied volatility moves by one percentage point.
Velocity of money
How often a unit of money changes hands in a period, linking the money supply to total spending.
VIX
The market's 'fear gauge': expected S&P 500 volatility over the next 30 days.
VIX futures
Futures contracts that settle to the value of the VIX on their expiry date.
Void period
A period when a rental property has no tenant and earns no rent.
Volatility
How much and how fast a price moves.
Volatility clustering
The tendency of large price moves to be followed by large moves, and small moves by small ones.
Volatility decay
The tendency of daily-reset leveraged and inverse funds to lose ground in choppy markets, because gains and losses compound on a changing base.
Volatility ETP
An exchange-traded product that holds VIX futures to give exposure to volatility, often with daily rolling and resetting.
Volatility persistence
The tendency for calm periods to follow calm periods and turbulent ones to follow turbulent ones.
Volatility risk premium
The tendency of implied volatility to exceed later realised volatility, which pays option sellers for bearing risk.
Volatility skew
The pattern of implied volatility across strike prices for the same expiry.
Volatility targeting
Scaling total exposure so the forecast portfolio volatility matches a chosen target. Exposure = target volatility / forecast volatility.
Volmageddon
The nickname for the 5 February 2018 volatility spike that wiped out several short volatility products.
Volume
The number of shares or contracts traded in a period.
Volume climax
An extreme burst of volume and range at the end of a long move. A selling climax follows a steep fall; a buying climax follows a long rally.
VWAP
Volume-weighted average price for the session, a benchmark used by intraday traders.

W

WACC
Weighted average cost of capital: the blended rate a company must pay its equity and debt providers, used to discount cash flows.
Walk-forward analysis
A testing method that repeatedly tunes a system on one window of data, then trades it unchanged on the next unseen window. It tests the tuning process, not just one setting.
Walk-forward testing
Repeatedly fitting a strategy on past data and trading it on the following period, rolling forward through time.
Walking the book
Filling a large order across several price levels of the order book, so the average price is worse than the quote.
Warrant
A long-dated option giving the right to buy or sell an asset at a fixed price before a set date.
WASDE
The USDA's monthly World Agricultural Supply and Demand Estimates report, a major event for grain markets.
WASDE report
The USDA's monthly World Agricultural Supply and Demand Estimates. It forecasts production, use and ending stocks for major crops.
Wash sale
A sale at a loss followed by a purchase of the same or a substantially identical security within 30 days, which disallows the loss for now.
Wash trade
A trade in which the buyer and seller are effectively the same party, creating false volume.
Watchlist
A short list of assets you follow closely, often grouped by how close each is to a trade.
Weighted score
A single number made by weighting and adding the scores of several factors, which a model then maps to a rating.
Whipsaw
A quick reversal that triggers a signal and then moves back, causing a small loss. Common for trend systems in ranging markets.
Whisper number
An unofficial expectation for a company's results, often above consensus for popular stocks. It can be the real bar the market wants cleared.
Withdrawal guardrails
Rules that cut or raise retirement spending when the portfolio moves beyond set limits.
Withholding tax
Tax a country takes at source on dividends paid to foreign investors. Tax treaties between countries can reduce the rate.
Worst-of basket
A basket of assets where the payoff depends on the weakest performer.
WTI and Brent
The two main crude oil benchmarks. WTI is US crude delivered at Cushing, Oklahoma; Brent is North Sea crude and the main reference for oil traded worldwide.

Y

Yield curve
A line showing bond yields from short to long maturities. Normally it slopes upward.
Yield on cost
The current dividend divided by the price you originally paid, showing how much your income has grown.
Yield to maturity (YTM)
The yearly return from buying a bond at today's price and holding it to maturity. It counts the coupons and the gain or loss to face value.
Yield to worst
For a callable bond, which the issuer can repay early, the lowest yield it can deliver across every possible call date and its maturity.
Yield trap
A share whose high yield exists because its price has fallen on doubts that the dividend can be maintained.

Z

Z-score
How many standard deviations a value is above or below its average. It is used to measure how stretched a price is.
